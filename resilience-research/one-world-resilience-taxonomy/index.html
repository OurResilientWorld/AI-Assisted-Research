One World Resilience Taxonomy

A map of the critical decisions democracies must make to build a more resilient world by 2035, and how the power to make them could move to citizens.

  • Working draft, November 2026
  • Evidence cut-off: 15 November 2026

Summary

Status: working draft. This page sets out a hypothesis and a design. It does not report findings. The list of decisions is a starting point for citizens to change.

Most resilience frameworks are written by governments and describe what governments should do. This taxonomy starts somewhere else. It asks what would have to be decided for the world's democracies to be more resilient by 2035, and who should hold those decisions.

It names 59 selected critical decisions across 13 systems, from energy and water to security and emergency response. For each one it shows who typically decides today, what a participatory step looks like, and how citizens would hold the decision in an active democracy by 2035.

My hypothesis is that democracies cannot build a resilient world unless they give more power to the people to make the critical decisions that building resilience requires. The taxonomy is built to test that hypothesis. Each decision is paired with a resilience outcome that does not depend on who governs, and the evidence against the hypothesis is recorded alongside the evidence for it.

Why this taxonomy starts from who decides

The first version of this taxonomy was built with AI from 44 international and national resilience frameworks. It was detailed and well sourced. It also kept government as the default decision-maker in almost every row, because most of its sources were governments describing what governments do.

That is the pattern I described in When AI reinforces business as usual: more detail, same framing.[14] So this version starts from two rules.

  1. Work backwards from 2035. Begin with the decisions a more resilient world requires, then ask who must hold them.
  2. Nothing may wait for a government to start it. Every decision and every enabler has a first step that households, communities, co-operatives or civil society can take now. Making it binding may still need Parliament, so the route and the fallback are stated.

The scope is democracies only. A country counts when V-Dem classes it as an electoral or liberal democracy, which was 87 countries at the end of 2025.[1]

Active democracy

The taxonomy describes a move through three stages. I use “active” and not “direct” democracy, because citizens do not make every decision. They hold the decisions that matter most, and they can check the rest.

StageCitizensBinding authority citizens holdRepresentatives and executives
Representative (today)Elect, and are consultedNone between electionsDecide
ParticipatoryDeliberate and adviseNone by right. A right to a reasoned response; any binding say is delegated and can be withdrawnDecide, and must answer
Active (2035)Hold four standing powers over the critical decisionsBinding by right, under standing rulesCarry out citizen decisions, run day-to-day government, and act in emergencies under agreed principles

The stages are shorthand. A real arrangement can mix them: a country can hold a veto by right on a matter where its citizens have no say over the agenda. A method does not define a stage either. A participatory budget that representatives grant and can take back is participatory, and one that citizens hold by right is active. By right means a later parliament cannot override it. A law that promises to follow a citizen vote, and that a later parliament could repeal, is still participatory.

Four citizen powers

PowerWhat it meansWhere part of it already works
Set the agenda CP1A standing body chosen by lot decides what goes to citizensThe Citizens' Council in Ostbelgien, Belgium[5]
Decide CP2A defined class of critical decisions must go to citizens, after deliberationIreland's assembly followed by a referendum; Swiss mandatory referendums[6][2]
Veto CP3Citizens can overturn a law or an executive decisionThe Swiss optional referendum[2]
Review CP4Affected citizens automatically review emergency and delegated decisionsNo national example found. This is a new design

No country holds all four powers. Switzerland comes closest on binding votes, with low turnout and little deliberation.[2][3] Ostbelgien has the standing council, with advisory powers and about 80,000 people.[5] Ireland joined deliberation to a binding vote.[6] Taiwan showed continuous digital participation, and Uruguay citizen-initiated votes.[7][8] Active democracy as defined here combines them.

Emergencies and black-swan events

There will be emergencies and events nobody predicted in which executives must decide. Moving those decisions to citizens might be possible by 2050. It is not possible by 2035. The taxonomy therefore applies one rule to 16 of the 59 decisions.

  1. Before: citizens adopt the principles that bind executives.
  2. During: executives decide. Speed is not traded for process.
  3. Limits: every declaration is time-limited, and citizens can end one by veto.
  4. After: the relevant citizens automatically review every emergency against the principles.

Every decision made in an emergency is within the review's reach. Major orders are examined one by one. Operational calls are examined by sample, and any affected citizen can refer a specific decision. The responsible body must answer each finding in public: accept it with a corrective action and a date, or give its reasons. A contested finding goes to the standing citizens' council, which can refer it to a citizen decision.

Two assumptions about 2035

The move to active democracy asks more of people's time. I make two assumptions about what will make that possible. Both are forecasts, and both will be checked against participation rates as pilots run.

A four-day working week. If AI replaces jobs faster than new jobs are created, a four-day working week is very likely to be common before 2035, and more free time is likely to encourage a higher rate of participation. The evidence that a shorter week is workable is growing: in a Deakin University study of 15 Australian firms, none reported a drop in productivity and 14 kept the arrangement.[15] What has not been shown is that the extra day turns into civic participation. The Parliamentary Library describes trial results as mixed, and our own earlier research found no causal evidence for that link.[16][19]

Online participation. Moving participation online would make it easier for people to take part. Estonia has run binding internet voting since 2005, and just over half of the votes in its 2023 parliamentary election were cast online.[17] No country votes online only. Switzerland halted its trials in 2019 over a security flaw before restarting them on a limited scale, and New South Wales stopped using its internet voting system after 2021.[17][18]

My decision is that participation will be online only by 2035, with offline channels kept only during the transition. An online-only system is a single point of failure for the democracy it serves, and it shuts out anyone without a device or a connection. Three safeguards are part of the design to manage that: a vote interrupted by an outage or attack is paused and extended, never cancelled; staffed public access points mean no one needs their own device; and the system is independently audited before each stage of expansion. These safeguards have not been designed or tested.

Citizens can pilot every one of these without a mandate. Making them bind is a separate step. 6 of the 15 need new law or constitutional change: standing citizens' councils, reserved critical decisions, citizens' veto, civic time and pay, duty to act, binding citizen vote. A further 5 need a mandate that existing powers may or may not supply: emergency principles charter, deliberate, then decide, automatic citizen review, rights floor, trusted participation infrastructure. For those, citizen action builds the evidence and the demand, and a legislature, an agency or a constitutional vote still has to give them force. In Australia there is no binding mechanism for citizens outside the formal constitutional referendum process. That has to change before 2035 if active democracy is to be possible, and it is named here as its own enabler: the binding citizen vote. This is the largest risk to the 2035 date, and a milestone only counts when the evidence, the mandate route and the funding behind it can be shown.

The role of AI

AI has two roles in this taxonomy, and neither is deciding.

Expert, in the IPCC sense. The IPCC does not tell governments what to do. It assesses the published evidence and says how confident it is. AI used as an expert follows the same rules: it assesses evidence without originating it, rates the evidence and the level of agreement, states its confidence in calibrated terms, shows the full range of outcomes, names what is not known, and never recommends a policy.[11] The plain-English summary is approved by the people who will use it.

Mediator, when a decision cannot be reached. A citizen body that is deadlocked can call in mediation. The AI sorts disputes about facts from disputes about values, maps the positions in people's own words, drafts common-ground statements that keep minority views, and sets out the remaining options and trade-offs. Then it hands back. The people decide.

Both roles run under the same safeguards: people decide; AI is invited in and can be dismissed; models, prompts and sources are disclosed; more than one model is used; anyone can challenge an output or ask for a human mediator; there is no profiling or persuasion; and the work is independently audited. In an emergency, AI supports executives with evidence and is not used to mediate.

The evidence for AI mediation is one promising study of 5,734 people in the United Kingdom.[12][13] I treat it as a design to test, in line with the caution in our earlier research on AI and democratic renewal.[19]

The lesson I take from the evidence against is that binding votes without good deliberation tend to block change. The design answers it with four enablers: deliberation before every binding vote, a rights floor, paid civic time, and a duty to act on citizen decisions.

How the hypothesis will be tested

A citizen process being adopted is not evidence of resilience. An energy pathway can be approved by citizen vote and the lights can still go out. So resilience is judged by four outcomes that are defined without reference to who governs.

OutcomeWhat it measuresHeadline measure
Continuity RO1Essential needs keep being met through a shockUnmet essential-need days
Recovery RO2How quickly services and lives are restoredTime to restore
Cost RO3Losses plus spending to prevent and respondTotal cost per event or per year
Distribution RO4Who carries the lossGap between the most and least affected groups

The hypothesis is tested through a narrower claim: for a given decision, giving citizens a specific power improves a specific outcome, compared with the arrangement it replaces. That can be checked one decision at a time, against a declared comparator, in the same place before and after or in matched places. For rare events it can be checked in exercises.

  • If the narrower claim holds across many decisions, it supports the hypothesis. It does not prove it.
  • If a democracy reaches its resilience outcomes without giving citizens more power, that challenges the hypothesis directly.
  • If one design for active democracy fails, that tests the design. It does not disprove the hypothesis.

No test has been run yet.

How to use the taxonomy

  • Communities and local groups: pick one decision that matters where you live and take its first step. Then look at which enablers it needs.
  • Groups working on different problems: use the shared list to see where your work connects. A standing citizens' council or an emergency principles charter helps water, housing and bushfire groups alike.
  • Independent representatives: choose a decision, run the participatory step openly with your community, and publish what you did with the result.
  • Researchers: test the hypothesis. Each decision has an outcome indicator in the data files, and the test design is set out above.
  • Simulations: a decision's stage can be a scenario setting in a model of pathways to 2035. It should not move a resilience outcome on its own. Any effect has to come through a stated mechanism with its evidence shown.

To correct something, add evidence or propose a decision that is missing, use the contribution forms.

Limits

  • Nothing here has been tested. The outcome indicators and the test design describe tests that have not been run.
  • The 59 decisions, their stages and the milestones are a first draft made by one author with AI assistance. No citizens' body has reviewed them.
  • Who takes part in each decision, who is affected without a vote, and how a decision is challenged depend on the jurisdiction and are not yet specified. Some decisions involve populations with competing claims, such as residents and their wider region.
  • The milestones show what would have to be true. No feasibility analysis has been done for any country.
  • The “Typical today” column generalises across democracies.
  • Most decisions have no research source for the mechanism proposed. Where a source is given, it shows which working model the mechanism was borrowed from.
  • The evidence for and against comes from a short review of five countries. It is not a literature review.
  • The two assumptions about 2035 are forecasts. No causal evidence was found that a shorter working week raises civic participation. No country votes online only, and its safeguards are untested.
  • Indicators in the data files are candidates and have not been checked against data.
  • The routes to binding authority are indicative. They vary by country and are not legal advice.
