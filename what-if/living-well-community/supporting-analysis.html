Living Well — proposal at a glance

Living Well would help volunteers aged 65–79 remain mobile, independent and connected by coordinating personal exercise/nutrition support, periodic professional review, a smartwatch and individual artificial intelligence (AI) coaching.

The proposed organisation is Living Well Inc., a new New South Wales (NSW) not-for-profit incorporated association. Members set strategic priorities and the budget framework, elect and scrutinise the management committee, and decide material expansion and commercialisation proposals. The committee remains legally accountable; paid management negotiates every partner arrangement.

A university partnership would cover initial research, pilot evaluation and separately funded long-term outcomes. A suitable university would be selected after feasibility work, considering relevant courses, research expertise, local reach, capacity, costs and acceptable partnership terms. No institution is nominated. Proposed terms protect research independence, participant data, continued community use of project outputs and fair benefit-sharing if research is later commercialised.

Startup crowdfunding is mandatory. The illustrative 100-person first year needs about $892,000 gross in 2026 dollars, or $932,000 at assumed 2027–28 prices, including formation/preparation, evaluation, reserves and a cash buffer. Private seed is required before paid work and included once in that target. No funds or partners are committed.

The most grounded expected benefit is improved or maintained everyday function for some participants; connection and confidence are plausible further benefits. Net family time, public savings and financial sustainability need separate evidence. Expansion follows comparative outcomes and funded capacity.

Read the concise four-part business case: Understand the Business Case; Implementing the Business Case; Managing the Program; The Outcomes. Detailed calculations and resilience tests remain in the supporting analysis. Viability is evaluated at the end, without changing the participant service to make the budget balance.

Living Well governance and delivery choices

A community-governed New South Wales (NSW) association

Recommend a new Living Well Inc., incorporated under the Associations Incorporation Act 2009 (NSW) with a custom not-for-profit constitution. The association owns the program, contracts with every partner, employs or contracts management and retains surplus for its purposes. Its proposed objects are older people's mobility, independence, connection and public-benefit learning. On winding up, remaining assets would go to an eligible similar-purpose not-for-profit, subject to applicable law and donor restrictions. No distribution to members is proposed.

This choice puts a local community purpose within a NSW legal structure. The participatory constitution, transparent practice and members' enforceable rights carry the philosophy; the legal label alone does not guarantee it. NSW guidance.

University research partnership and future commercialisation

Selecting a suitable university partner

Living Well would seek a university offering suitable courses and research capability in exercise, nutrition, healthy ageing and evaluation. No university is nominated at this stage. The appropriate partner will depend on the pilot location, community needs, available staff and facilities, research interests, costs and agreement on responsibilities and rights.

After local feasibility work, Living Well would prepare a common partnership brief and assess interested universities against:

  • relevant teaching and research expertise, with a named research lead and access to trial design, health economics and longitudinal research skills;
  • practical local reach, accessible participant involvement and capacity to support initial research and longer follow-up;
  • scientific independence, ethics and data safeguards, and willingness to report all findings;
  • full costs, available resources, timing and a sustainable funding arrangement;
  • acceptable intellectual-property, community-use, publication and future commercialisation terms.

The management committee would assess proposals within the member-approved direction and budget framework before authorising an agreement. If no suitable arrangement is available, revise the research plan or timing before making commitments. No university or academic has been contacted for this proposal.

University partnership covers research from design to long-term outcomes. Clinical assessment, professional review, safety decisions and referrals remain with qualified service providers. Students may contribute through separately approved, supervised placements or research projects. Consent, competence, supervision and educational obligations must be agreed; student participation is neither free core staffing nor a reason to add universal dietitian appointments.

Research program and funding gates

Living Well's members help choose meaningful questions and interpret practical implications. The university controls scientific methods and analysis within the agreed protocol, with independent methods review where its team also designed the intervention. A research steering group includes participant representatives, Living Well, university investigators and an independent methods/ethics adviser. Donors, suppliers and prospective licensees cannot suppress findings or determine clinical care.

Separate agreement before any commercialisation

Agree the process at research inception; approve a transaction only once the actual opportunity, rights and risks are known. The agreement must apply to commercial use by the university, Living Well or a third party of joint project outputs and Living Well background contributions. It does not give Living Well control of unrelated university research.

  1. Identify the invention/software/service, owners, third-party rights and contributors. Obtain a written public-benefit and financial appraisal, with independent advice where an investigator, committee member or supplier could benefit.
  2. Members deliberate and decide Living Well's material commercialisation proposal; the committee verifies legality and solvency; authorised officers of both institutions execute the licence. Student/inventor rights and any other owners' permissions must be resolved.
  3. Prefer non-exclusive licensing where practical. Any exclusivity needs a justified field, territory and term, access commitments, development milestones and termination/reversion for non-performance. Protect Living Well's continuing community-use licence and research/teaching rights.
  4. Define patent, development and licensing costs; which deductions are allowed; contributions; revenue-sharing formula; payment timing; audit rights and dispute handling. Negotiate a fair share rather than inventing a percentage now. Living Well reinvests its net proceeds in its not-for-profit purposes; private distributions to its members are excluded.
  5. Set affordable-access commitments and public-interest safeguards. Retain publication rights and time-limited IP review. Commercial use of personal or linked data is not granted merely by licensing an algorithm: separately check consent, ethics, privacy and necessary approvals or renewed consent.
  6. Review association eligibility, tax, charity/fundraising restrictions and conflicts before execution. Any new entity or change of structure requires a separate member decision and legal advice; no automatic spin-out is proposed.

Account 4 — public effects and usable capacity

Private cash receipts do not prove zero public fiscal effects. Record additional consultations, tests, care needs and any lawful tax concessions separately. Do not finance required programme delivery through hidden public payments. Public savings are not estimated or available as operator cash in this case.

Record clinical and coordination time by profession, location and timing, including programme governance and care induced by detecting needs. Aggregate service budgets do not establish a staffing roster. Usable clinical capacity equals attributable avoided compatible care hours multiplied by the share actually redeployable, less programme hours and induced demand. If programme hours are H and induced hours J for n people, avoided hours per person needed for a positive balance exceed (H+J)/(n×redeployable share), when that share is positive. This decline is not a survival forecast and should not be presented as observed attrition.

At an assumed 100 later-care hours per added person-year and $80/hour, the extra care is 5,000 hours/$400,000. These are arbitrary stress inputs to reveal a possible trade-off, not an estimated effect. No payer or public/private split is assumed for this care. Post-79 ordinary care needs would exist in both arms; the calculation is incremental hypothetical additional care, not all later-life costs.

A complete lifetime model would need age/sex/baseline-function transitions, survival, disability, care intensity, family composition, quality of life and service cost data with an explicit comparator. They are absent. The sensitivity therefore cannot establish net lifetime social value, prove LW-R05 or support a national benefit estimate. Observe families after support ends and participants pass 79; do not select only survivors with complete records.

Who may gain or lose across generations

Older people may gain independent choice and social participation; adult children may gain time for work, education, rest or their own families. Grandchildren may receive additional care or simply more enjoyable contact. These are different outcomes and not automatic productive labour. Women already providing substantial unpaid care could receive more duties instead of relief. Younger families might pay fees, provide transport, troubleshoot devices or inherit later care work. Measure household bills and time by person, including those not enrolled.

Private donor and insurer funds have alternatives: disability support, younger-family prevention, accessible existing exercise or direct home assistance. A $1 million programme cannot also buy the alternative service. Make the comparator budget and workforce explicit; do not assume private spending has no social opportunity cost. Foregone tax receipts from any deductible gifts or premium arrangements belong in the public ledger, not an invisible subsidy. No lifetime net benefit is booked now.

Other countries, technology and supply chains

At pilot scale, direct international benefit is likely limited and is not valued. Reproducible methods and honest negative findings could eventually inform other countries, subject to separate sharing/publication approval; Australian cost or clinical results would require local revalidation. Migrant families may have cross-border caring obligations, so diaries should capture them voluntarily without assuming national benefit from all hours.

Imported watches/batteries, software updates, cloud regions, AI inference, support staff, undersea connectivity and vendor solvency create foreign dependencies. Supplier nationality does not establish processing location or legal access. Require auditable contracts, complete data-flow testing, exportable plans, local encrypted backup, a replacement-device reserve, support exit clauses and staffed offline continuity. Local manufacture or Australian ownership is not assumed. Device energy use and electronic waste are plausible external costs; request lifecycle/recycling evidence rather than inventing emissions savings.

The international dependencies above require local evidence; this case asserts no national self-sufficiency or international resilience improvement.
