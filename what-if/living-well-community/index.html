What if communities funded Living Well?

A practical local program to help older Australians stay mobile, independent and involved in the lives they choose.

Understand the Business Case

The problem: people can lose confidence, mobility and social connection when useful support is difficult to find, afford or coordinate. Living Well would bring that support together around each person's goals.

Why this matters

For adults aged 65–79, everyday independence means walking to the shops, managing at home and spending time with family and friends. Declining strength, balance or confidence can narrow those choices. Transport, cost, disability, language and digital barriers can make available services harder to use.

Living Well would start locally in 2027, with voluntary participation and a defined pilot. Its purpose is better daily life, not compulsory exercise targets or an expectation that older people provide unpaid care.

How the proposal could help: easier access → a suitable personal plan → sustained activity and support → greater everyday independence. Each link needs testing.

What every participant receives

A suitable professional assesses the person's needs and agrees a tailored exercise and practical nutrition plan. Periodic professional review adapts that plan. Everyone receives a supported smartwatch and individual artificial intelligence (AI) coaching, with a named human contact and accessible phone or written help. The professional determines suitable review frequency and referrals; there is no universal extra appointment package.

The watch and AI help people follow the agreed plan. Clinicians make clinical decisions. Family involvement is optional. Local activity and social contact complement the technology.

Why a coordinated program?

Regular encouragement and easier access may help people continue activities they value. Evidence supports exercise components, including fall prevention, but does not prove the combined Living Well offer. Compare it with current services and with spending the same resources on improving access to existing support. Exercise evidence.

Implementing the Business Case

The approach: establish Living Well, fund a complete local pilot through an essential crowdfunding campaign, then expand only when benefits, delivery capacity and continuing finance justify it.

From formation to a funded pilot

Sequence: seed underwriting → establish Living Well and cost delivery → crowdfunding → readiness check → 12-month pilot → university evaluation and renewal decision. A failed funding or safety gate means delay or redesign.

  1. Prepare and form. Secure private seed underwriting before paid formation and feasibility work. Test local need, compare suppliers and confirm the legal structure. Founders record responsibility for any pre-incorporation costs; the new organisation takes on only approved obligations.
  2. Negotiate and fund. Living Well obtains full-cost proposals and conditional arrangements, then runs the required crowdfunding campaign. Explain who receives the service, what donations cover, how funds are protected and what happens if the target is missed.
  3. Deliver safely. Begin a proposed 100-person, 12-month pilot only after cleared funds, signed contracts, clinical/data safeguards and continuity arrangements are in place. Research consent is separate from receiving support.
  4. Evaluate and renew. Review participation, function, safety, access, actual costs and willingness to continue. Future fees or subsidies are explained before enrolment. Fund renewal or a supported handover before the pilot ends.

What startup would cost

The proposed New South Wales (NSW) association uses planning allowances, not supplier quotes. These figures cover 100 people, one year and the same participant services, in 2026 Australian dollars. A 10% reserve is already included in service and startup amounts; the separate buffer is held cash.

The target is about $932,000 at assumed 2027–28 prices, using 3% annual inflation. Private seed of $66,000 includes early formation costs and is included once in startup costs and reimbursed after a successful campaign; the underwriter bears the agreed loss if it fails. No funding is committed. The $120,000 research allowance within startup is a placeholder for initial/pilot work; university quotations may change the target. Long-term research needs separate secured funding.

The pilot illustration has no participant fees or duplicate sponsor receipts. Crowdfunding is essential; it does not establish a permanent income stream. Continuing funding could combine affordable participant payments, funded concessions, sponsors or recurring donors. At the tested prices, ongoing finance still needs work. Full calculations and overhead sensitivities are in the financial analysis.

Delivery decisions through the decade

Begin with one local service network, potentially covering metropolitan and nearby regional participants. July 2027 is an earliest illustration, not a deadline that overrides readiness. Test effectiveness over 2028–30 before substantial expansion. The financial scenario grows from 100 to 1,000 participants through 2036–37; it is a planning exercise, not forecast demand.

Managing the Program

Recommendation: establish Living Well Inc., a new not-for-profit incorporated association under the Associations Incorporation Act 2009 (NSW). Members set its direction; an elected management committee is legally accountable; paid management negotiates and manages all partner arrangements.

Community decisions with clear responsibility

The constitution would put One World principles into practice: voluntary cooperation, open evidence, collective priorities and scrutiny of power. Membership is optional and separate from receiving care or joining research. Each member has one vote; wealth, donations and professional status buy no extra votes.

Members propose → examine evidence and alternatives → decide priorities → committee and staff deliver → members review results. Clinicians retain authority over safe care; university researchers retain independence over findings.

Members elect and may remove the committee, approve strategic priorities and the annual budget framework, and decide material expansion or commercialisation proposals after accessible deliberation. They can request reasons and an independent review. The committee checks legality, safety and solvency, explains any decision it cannot implement, and returns alternatives to members. Management handles routine contracts within approved limits. Urgent protective action proceeds immediately, with recorded review within 30 days.

Phone, paper and supported meetings must complement digital participation. Publish decision reasons, conflicts, financial summaries and evidence while protecting personal information. AI may organise evidence; people decide. Proposed rules and statutory responsibilities are detailed in the governance and delivery plan.

Establish Living Well and choose delivery

Founders secure seed backing, develop a custom constitution with prospective members and obtain formation advice. After registration, the committee appoints management and a clinical lead, establishes controlled accounts and insurance, and arranges the required campaign. Paid capacity must be costed; role names do not imply full-time posts.

Delivery arrangement under Living Well Assessment
Contracted local provider network — recommended for pilot Uses existing qualified capacity; requires reliable coordination and continuity contracts
Employed multidisciplinary team More direct control; higher recruitment and fixed-cost exposure
Small employed coordination/clinical core plus contracted local teams Potential expansion option; test supervision and geographic coverage

All provide the same participant offer and university research partnership. Obtain comparable full-cost proposals before choosing; the model does not price these three arrangements separately.

One organisation, clear partner agreements

Living Well negotiates every arrangement: participant goals and consent; qualified providers' care and escalation; accessible community venues and transport; watch/AI suppliers' privacy and human fallback; donors' and funders' payment restrictions; and the university's research program. Independent complaints, legal and financial assurance remain available. No partner is committed.

A university partnership from the beginning

Living Well would seek a university partner with suitable courses and research capability in exercise, nutrition, healthy ageing and evaluation. Selection would follow local feasibility work and depend on location, available expertise, capacity, costs and mutually acceptable terms. No university is nominated at this stage.

Living Well and the university would co-design initial research with participants, establish baseline measures, evaluate the pilot and build funded long-term follow-up. Qualified providers remain responsible for care. Student involvement requires supervision and consent; it does not add mandatory dietitian appointments or replace paid clinical capacity.

Before research begins, sign an agreement covering costs, ethics, optional research consent, data protection, independent analysis and publication of all findings. Agree existing and new intellectual-property rights, continued community use and the process for later commercialisation. Any commercial licence needs a separate agreement covering fair benefit-sharing, affordable access, conflicts and exit; Living Well's proceeds support its purpose. No research income or royalties are assumed. See the partnership and agreement proposal.

The Outcomes

The most credible successful outcome is modest improvement or maintenance of everyday function for some participants, alongside easier access to support and greater confidence to stay involved. Large health-system savings or self-funding growth are much less certain.

What is reasonably expected?

These are reasoned expectations if people receive suitable care and continue using it, not numerical probability forecasts or measured Living Well results.

Evidence ladder: component evidence supports better function and fewer falls; added connection and confidence are plausible; reduced family burden, released professional capacity and financial self-sufficiency need separate proof. Outcomes will differ between people.

  • Most grounded expectation: suitable sustained exercise can improve balance, strength and mobility and reduce falls in some older populations. The combined program's added benefit over existing support remains to be measured. LIFE mobility trial and falls review inform this expectation; study populations and interventions differ from Living Well.
  • Plausible additional benefit: a named contact, practical help and chosen local activity may support confidence, wellbeing and family/community involvement. Technology helps only if it adds useful support without excessive burden.
  • More uncertain: relatives may need to provide less unwanted assistance, but participation and technology can also demand their time. Reduced hospital use, net workforce capacity, enduring intergenerational benefits and national resilience gains cannot be assumed.

What would justify expansion?

The university research team compares the full program with current services and, in a later properly designed study, equivalent professional support without the watch/AI package. Assess walking, balance, daily tasks, personal goals, wellbeing, safety, access and family time. Track the full resources used and who bears the costs.

Measure at baseline, six and twelve months; agree separately funded follow-up at 24 months and 3–5 years, with a decade extension conditional on resources and consent. Agree a useful improvement threshold before recruitment; report uncertainty and missing outcomes. Expand only when there is credible additional benefit, acceptable safety and access, paid delivery capacity, funded continuing obligations and reliable support during heat, workforce or technology disruption. A successful appeal alone is insufficient. See the evaluation plan.

Evaluation of viability

Worth developing into a costed feasibility decision; not yet demonstrated viable. The association has not been formed, donor demand is unknown and costs need full quotations. NSW association suitability for this service, later interstate growth and commercial activity needs legal confirmation; national expansion is not automatic. Charity registration and tax-deductible gifts are separate. NSW association guidance. The illustrative ongoing member/sponsor model leaves a $181,375 annual shortfall for 250 newly recruited places under the recommended structure. Continuing participants cost less, but hypothetical later surpluses cannot pay an earlier bill.

The decision is whether the intended benefits warrant testing the proposition and its funding demand. If safe delivery, affordable renewal or useful comparative benefit cannot be demonstrated, redesign or stop expansion and provide the funded handover. Do not turn wider social value into operator revenue or describe conditional national effects as achieved results.
