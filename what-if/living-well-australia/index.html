Overview

Help older Australians stay mobile, independent and involved in family and community life. The proposal combines professional support, suitable exercise, better diet, an optional watch and an offer of individual artificial intelligence (AI) coaching.

The proposed May 2027 Budget would fund a July 2027 start. The first budget covers 2027–28 to 2030–31. The ten-year outlook covers 2027–28 to 2036–37, including those first four years. The four-year structure follows the Parliamentary Budget Office’s explanation of the budget year and three forward years.

What the money means

Government savings are reductions in public care and research bills. Wider benefits also recognise preserved unpaid care, childcare, volunteering and usable professional time. The wider total includes government savings once; these two figures must not be added together.

The proposal’s purpose is better lives. The funding decision is whether those improvements justify the additional public spending, and whether a pilot demonstrates a safe, useful and affordable service. The current model does not establish that care savings pay for the programme.

Four alternatives

  • Pathway 1 — existing services. Continue current services as the comparison; no new campaign.
  • Pathway 2 — checks and support. Offer preventive checks more widely, with supported enrolment where useful.
  • Pathway 3 — pilot, then steady growth. Evaluate a smaller two-year pilot before expanding.
  • Pathway 4 — larger campaign. Start with 10,000 funded places in selected areas; reach 3.5% of the eligible age group by June 2035, then maintain that share to June 2037.

Key points about the estimates

This is a proposal, not an approved government programme. The ranges contain the middle 80% of model runs under stated assumptions; they are not promises or confidence limits. Existing research supports the health purpose, but programme-specific savings and unpaid-work effects need evaluation. Unpaid time cannot pay government invoices, and wellbeing, participant time and some longer-term effects remain unpriced. The model includes a 10% funding reserve and separate weak-effect, no-benefit and adverse tests. Each pathway is an alternative, so their totals are not added.

The programme

Why offer it?

People want to keep walking, managing daily tasks, seeing friends and helping others. Suitable activity, practical nutrition support and continuing encouragement can help them pursue those goals. The programme would connect that support around the person, rather than leave them to assemble it alone.

Who can join?

The offer is voluntary for Australians aged 65–79, regardless of pension eligibility. Begin with people living in the community, including people with disabilities and existing health conditions, using an appropriate individual plan. Recruitment should reach people facing language, transport, financial or digital barriers. Arrange a handover to suitable services when someone turns 80 or leaves.

What happens for a participant?

  1. Agree a plan. A professional reviews health, medicines, mobility, strength, diet, wellbeing and practical barriers. The participant chooses achievable goals, including suitable strength, balance and aerobic activity.
  2. Choose the support. Local activities, appointments and telephone or written support remain available. A watch and AI coach are optional; declining either, or optional research, does not remove access to the core service.
  3. Get help between visits. The coach explains the agreed plan, offers reminders and suggests approved alternatives. It refers symptoms and concerning trends to a human; it does not change medicines or invent treatment.
  4. Review what is working. The participant and professional review progress and adapt the plan. Being active means continuing engagement with the agreed support, not simply wearing a watch or meeting one step target.
  5. Stay in control. Participants choose whether to join optional research and can stop new research collection. Staff explain record-retention obligations and the limits of withdrawing findings already published in aggregate.

How the service would be organised

A proposed national coordinating team would set service standards, fund local delivery and arrange independent clinical, statistical and privacy oversight. Local providers would deliver assessments and support, with clear referral routes to existing care. Expansion would follow review of safety, health outcomes, access, retention and delivery quality.

The pilot would compare supported participants with existing services and test AI-assisted support against equivalent human support. Collect mobility, strength, falls, wellbeing, care use and unpaid help given and received at the start and follow-up reviews. Publish the evaluation plan before seeing results. Use consented health-record linkage to examine care use, rather than infer it from steps.

Keep personal information under control

Keep identifiable records within approved programme infrastructure. Separate research identifiers from identity keys; give staff and agencies only the access needed for their authorised roles. Publish checked group findings. Test watch, phone, server, logging, updates and support connections for unwanted data transfers before using personal records.

Use the requested non-Chinese model-provider condition alongside checks of model provenance, licensing and the complete data system. Open-weight models that can run inside controlled infrastructure are candidates for testing; no vendor is selected. Programme code may be open source while personal information stays private.

How data would support improvement

Watches can supply activity, pulse and sleep estimates; clinical checks and participant reports supply other information. Approved analysts compare groups by age, sex, starting health, disability and location, accounting for missing data and unequal uptake. Promising patterns become research questions for controlled evaluation before new advice is adopted. Device studies such as the Apple Heart Study show how remote data collection and follow-up can be organised. They do not establish the effectiveness of this whole programme.

Design limits

A watch is not a diagnosis, and the coach supplements professional care. Removing names does not guarantee anonymity; access controls and continuing privacy assessment remain necessary. Residential-care delivery needs a separately tested design. These boundaries follow the proposed service design and Australian privacy guidance.

Anticipated health outcomes

Keep moving and manage daily life. In the Lifestyle Interventions and Independence for Elders trial, about 301 of every 1,000 people receiving structured activity lost the ability to walk 400 metres, compared with 355 receiving health education over an average 2.6 years. The participants were sedentary adults aged 70–89 at risk of disability. Original trial.

Reduce falls through balance and functional exercise. A Cochrane review found about 23% fewer falls compared with control programmes. Its example is 195 fewer falls where 1,000 people would otherwise experience 850 falls in a year. This supports appropriate exercise design; it is not a forecast of this programme’s event counts. Cochrane review.

Manage ongoing health needs. Diet, suitable activity and professional review aim to improve risk management and day-to-day wellbeing. Track blood pressure, blood-sugar risk where relevant, mental wellbeing, medicines and treatment use; no additional condition-specific event counts are assumed.

Stay involved and need less help. The programme aims to preserve confidence, social participation and the ability to give and receive care on better terms. Measure these directly in the pilot. No extra dollar value for wellbeing or number of healthy life-years is invented here.

How to read the benefit ranges

These are conditional additional benefits compared with existing services. A participant-year is one person supported for one year: per-person figures divide cumulative benefits by the sum of annual active places, not unique people or money paid to them. Pathway 2 averages also include its separate check-only activity. Row means add to totals; range endpoints do not, because each total is calculated within the same simulation run. Public savings are a subset of care resources freed; the remaining capacity, unpaid services and professional time are valued separately. National unpaid-work levels do not establish the programme’s assumed 5–15% preservation effect. Age Pension changes, new-treatment discoveries and unvalidated benefits beyond participants receive no credit. No benefits are credited after exit or beyond June 2037.

Sources and calculation trail

Annual service costs are reconstructed from retained Australian Institute of Health and Welfare spending, government budget inputs and age profiles. The model holds real age-specific costs per resident fixed, then applies each financial year's age population and active participation. Commonwealth grants are counted once by original funder. Health spending source; age projections.

For each service: baseline cost × participant share × participant-needs adjustment × time-to-benefit factor × assumed service-cost change. Positive resource savings are multiplied by a budget-release fraction; extra spending is charged in full. Research collection is counted once. The entries below record the retained mature effect assumptions, not observed Australian programme effects.

Public hospital admissions: service-cost reduction -5% to 12%; full effect after 2 years; 25%–75% of positive savings releases budget. Look AHEAD hospital-cost reduction of 10% supports an optimistic reduction; LIFE found more admissions. The -5% to 12% band is an assessment range, not either trial interval.

Emergency departments: service-cost reduction -3% to 12%; full effect after 2 years; 25%–75% of positive savings releases budget. Fewer falls and acute complications may reduce visits; increased detection can raise use. No precise all-cause cost effect is established.

Public hospital clinics: service-cost reduction -5% to 10%; full effect after 2 years; 25%–75% of positive savings releases budget. Fewer complications and repeat visits, offset by newly identified need. REACT suggests lower other-hospital use but does not establish this Australian rate.

Public funding of private hospital treatment: service-cost reduction -5% to 12%; full effect after 2 years; 50%–100% of positive savings releases budget. Same clinical mechanism as admissions; includes only direct public funding. Insurance rebates are a separate zero-saving row.

General practitioner visits: service-cost reduction -8% to 6%; full effect after 2 years; 100%–100% of positive savings releases budget. More prevention and follow-up can initially outweigh fewer chronic-care visits. REACT primary-care costs were similar between groups.

Specialist consultations: service-cost reduction -5% to 10%; full effect after 2 years; 100%–100% of positive savings releases budget. Fewer complications and better chronic-disease control may reduce repeat specialist visits; new diagnoses can add visits.

Laboratory tests: service-cost reduction -10% to 8%; full effect after 2 years; 100%–100% of positive savings releases budget. Less disease monitoring competes with extra testing after detection. No direct long-term trial estimate for this separate Australian spending row.

Scans and X-rays: service-cost reduction -5% to 10%; full effect after 2 years; 100%–100% of positive savings releases budget. Fewer injuries or complications may avoid scans; referral and detection can increase use. No precise trial-to-budget transfer.

Subsidised medicines: service-cost reduction -3% to 10%; full effect after 3 years; 100%–100% of positive savings releases budget. Look AHEAD found 7% lower medication costs, while diabetes prevention reduces future need. The 10% upper case tests a broader combined offer; it is not a measured whole-cohort effect.

Allied health, including psychology: service-cost reduction -10% to 5%; full effect after 2 years; 50%–100% of positive savings releases budget. Better mental and physical health can reduce treatment, but appropriate therapy may increase. Programme-funded sessions are excluded from this row.

Community health services: service-cost reduction -5% to 8%; full effect after 2 years; 25%–75% of positive savings releases budget. Better self-management and mental health may reduce ongoing support, while better access increases unmet-need treatment.

Public dental support: service-cost reduction 0% to 0%; full effect after 1 years; 0%–0% of positive savings releases budget. No specific oral-health intervention or defensible direct budget effect identified; assess no saving rather than omit the spending.

Ambulance and patient transport: service-cost reduction -5% to 12%; full effect after 2 years; 25%–75% of positive savings releases budget. Linked to fewer emergencies and treatment journeys; service capacity may remain funded.

Aids and appliances: service-cost reduction -3% to 6%; full effect after 3 years; 50%–100% of positive savings releases budget. Preserved mobility may delay aids, but new assessment can reveal unmet need. Programme watches are separately costed.

Population public-health services: service-cost reduction 0% to 0%; full effect after 1 years; 0%–0% of positive savings releases budget. Vaccination, surveillance and other shared services remain necessary; no exercise-linked reduction credited.

General health administration: service-cost reduction 0% to 0%; full effect after 1 years; 0%–0% of positive savings releases budget. No evidence that this small campaign reduces system-wide administration budgets. Programme AI capacity is a separate benefit.

Government health research: service-cost reduction 0% to 0%; full effect after 1 years; 0%–100% of positive savings releases budget. Use a separate avoided-collection formula: 0, 50% or 100% of 2/3/5 otherwise-funded studies, each reusing 1,000 records at $600 avoided collection cost.

Private health insurance rebates: service-cost reduction 0% to 0%; full effect after 1 years; 0%–0% of positive savings releases budget. Fewer claims do not automatically reduce government premium subsidies. No premium/rebate change is assumed.

Residential and flexible aged care: service-cost reduction -2% to 8%; full effect after 5 years; 10%–50% of positive savings releases budget. Preserved function might delay entry or intensity. Apply only half the whole-age average cost to community-living participants; long-term entry effects are not established.

Home care and support: service-cost reduction -5% to 10%; full effect after 3 years; 25%–75% of positive savings releases budget. Improved function may reduce support needs; better identification can increase appropriate care. Do not substitute unpaid family care for public care.

Restorative and transition care: service-cost reduction -10% to 8%; full effect after 3 years; 25%–75% of positive savings releases budget. Rehabilitation use may rise even as later dependence falls. This is a separate service bill, not another label for hospital savings.

Aged-care assessment, access and quality: service-cost reduction -5% to 2%; full effect after 3 years; 10%–50% of positive savings releases budget. Referrals may initially raise assessments. Only a small upper reduction is allowed; programme onboarding is separately funded.

Additional own-funded council support: service-cost reduction -3% to 8%; full effect after 3 years; 0%–50% of positive savings releases budget. Less need for practical support could release some resources; the baseline and effect are both weakly measured. Government grants and existing council health spending are excluded.

How to read the cost ranges

The ranges contain the middle 80% of simulated funding needs, rather than the full possible cost envelope or supplier quotes. Per-person means per participant-year, including a share of setup, common services and reserve; Pathway 2 also includes check-only services. Totals are undiscounted budget sums. “Future dollars” assumes 3% annual price growth from 2026; “2026 dollars” removes that assumption for comparison. No official inflation forecast, procurement price or appropriation is claimed. The reserve is a funding allowance, not a prediction that every dollar will be spent. Full-year places are funded; slower in-year recruitment could change costs and benefits.

Unit allowances and annual calculation
  • Clinical/community support per active year: $750–$1,250 in 2026 dollars.
  • AI operation per active year: $90–$240 in 2026 dollars.
  • Secure data per active year: $60–$160 in 2026 dollars.
  • Assessment/onboarding per new enrolment: $750–$1,250 in 2026 dollars.
  • Watch per new enrolment or replacement: $300–$600 in 2026 dollars.
  • Additional preventive check: $250–$450 in 2026 dollars.

Fixed annual operations / initial setup, in 2026 dollars: Pathway 2 $4–8m / $10–18m; Pathway 3 $6–12m / $12–24m; Pathway 4 $8–18m / $15–30m. Add 10% once to the sum of all components. Watch funding is available for every active participant even though use is optional.

New enrolments equal this year’s active places minus retained prior-year participants. Annual continuation ranges from 70% to 90%, centred on 80%. Watches are replaced every three years only for the surviving enrolment cohort. Setup appears only in 2027–28. Budgeted places are full-year equivalents; benefits from new enrolments begin at half-year maturity.

The price factor is 1.03 raised to (financial-year end minus 2026 minus 0.5): a midpoint adjustment. No discounting is applied to cumulative budget sums. Ages 65–69, 70–74 and 75–79 follow the retained official projections through June 2037, with equal uptake by age.

What these results can and cannot tell us

The central range contains 80% of these model runs. It is conditional on analyst-selected distributions, not an 80% guarantee or a measured probability of programme success. Frequencies of covering costs refer only to those runs. Draws share health and cost drivers; the same uncertain parameter persists across years, so ten-year uncertainty is not artificially reduced by treating each year as an independent experiment. The separate tests expose weak or adverse assumptions. The wider balance excludes unpriced wellbeing, participant effort, some harms and lifetime consequences, so it is not a complete social cost–benefit verdict.

Simulation method, assumptions and reproducibility

Triangular distributions use a lower limit, most likely value and upper limit. A shared health driver links service effects, contribution and benefit speed; a separate cost driver links unit costs and shared operations. The normal latent correlation is 0.5, tested at 0 and 0.8. Continue each parameter draw across the full ten years. End-year age populations set the annual age mix; benefit effects ramp by surviving enrolment cohorts over 1, 2, 3 or 5 years.

Primary seed: 20261109; 100,000 runs per pathway. An independent seed uses 50,000 runs. The model also tests uniform rather than triangular distributions, weaker unpaid-work preservation (0/5/15%), zero and adverse contribution, lower time valuation, and no credited benefit. Recruitment targets are fixed planning conditions, not forecasts of take-up. Baseline care and unpaid value vary by 0.8–1.2 and participant profiles by 0.5–1.5.

For each run, add yearly amounts first, then calculate the 10th and 90th percentiles and the average. The same calculation supplies every summary and detail table. Do not add row percentile endpoints or annual percentiles to obtain an overall interval.
