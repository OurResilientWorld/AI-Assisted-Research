1 · The research question

Could Australia more than double its output per hour by 2035 if AI capabilities advance exponentially and the country simultaneously transforms health, housing, energy, industry and public administration?

High-upside hypothesis, not a forecast

The fourth scenario is intentionally far beyond normal institutional projections. It explores an outcome worth testing, not an outcome already demonstrated. Favourable GDP arithmetic alone cannot establish the availability of skilled workers, capital, electricity, resilient housing, social consent or fair distribution.

+0.5%Observed 2025–26 real GDP per hour, ABS
1.2%Treasury long-term productivity assumption (AI included)
12%Australian businesses reporting AI use, 2024–25
6–8%ATSE’s modelled incremental GDP from AI by 2034

The main evidence for examining an exceptional case is exponential improvement in AI task capability, measured by METR; it does not imply the same exponential improvement in whole-economy output. Separately, CEPR researchers have modelled an extreme AI case with GDP growth reaching 15% per year by 2030, accompanied by serious labour displacement. Such work demonstrates why distribution and transition costs must be examined alongside GDP.

Modelling principle: GDP per capita and output per hour remain the core economic results. Housing affordability, care outcomes, distribution, emissions and trust are separate pass/fail measures, not invented as dollar multipliers.

2 · Four 2035 pathways

1. Business as usual

Gradual diffusion, limited institutional redesign and mounting housing, demographic and climate pressures. Closest in spirit to Treasury’s existing assumptions, but not the official Treasury forecast.

  • Underlying non-AI labour-productivity growth: 0.9%/yr
  • AI additional output/hour by 2035: +2.5%
  • Hours per resident: −2.5% relative to 2026

2. Reform and diffusion

Better skills, business dynamism, targeted capital investment and faster adoption of existing technology. Still recognisable as a conventional productivity-reform programme.

  • Underlying non-AI growth: 1.3%/yr
  • AI additional output/hour: +10%
  • Hours per resident: −1.2%

3. Integrated renewal

AI, housing construction, prevention-first health, care redesign, climate adaptation, infrastructure and institutional reform are treated as one programme.

  • Underlying non-AI growth: 1.8%/yr
  • AI additional output/hour: +30%
  • Hours per resident: +1.2%

4. Exponential AI + system transformation MOST OPTIMISTIC

AI capability compounds rapidly while Australia reorganises many industries, manages displacement, industrialises housing, expands healthy working years and reinforces critical systems.

  • Underlying non-AI growth: 2.2%/yr
  • Additional realised AI output/hour: +90% by 2035
  • Hours per resident: +4% through health, care and participation
  • Capability stress test: six-month doubling—not a proven nine-year extrapolation

All pathway assumptions beyond observed statistics are scenario inputs created for this exploratory model. The underlying non-AI rate includes the potential contribution of non-AI innovation and productive capital. The AI uplift is measured relative to the model’s non-AI counterfactual, avoiding an additional AI increment on top of Treasury’s AI-inclusive 1.2% rate.

4 · What produces growth, and what does not

AI capability

Exponential task-horizon index: 2^(12×years/doubling_months). Benchmarked progress is domain-specific; a capability doubling is not a GDP doubling.

Adoption + productive use

An S-shaped diffusion factor and a deliberately small translation elasticity turn capability into a bounded scenario-specified uplift in domestic output/hour.

Social + physical systems

Labour hours/person incorporate ageing, health and care. Climate effects reduce output/hour. Capital and organisational redesign sit within non-AI productivity and realised AI gains.

Real GDP = (2026 real GDP) × (GDP per hour index) × (hours worked per resident index) × (population index)

How the optimistic case is calculated: First, non-AI output/hour grows at the chosen rate. Second, AI supplies a separate, cumulative productivity multiplier that reaches the selected value in 2035; its path depends on exponential capability and S-shaped organisational deployment. Third, a scenario-specific climate discount is applied. Finally, output/hour is multiplied by hours/person and population.

Capital is not added again on the spending side of GDP. Investments are required to make gains possible, but this first-pass model does not yet endogenise depreciation, capital stocks, foreign ownership, energy systems or financing. Extra investment therefore appears in the feasibility tests below and is not subtracted automatically from GDP. “GDP above BAU” in the table denotes the annual 2035 difference, not cumulative 2026–35 output or spendable government revenue.

The hard economic question: In pathway 4, realised AI uplift of 90% to 2035 is many times larger than ATSE’s measured-framework estimate of 6–8% additional Australian GDP by 2034. It requires breakthroughs in both AI applications and complementary systems, especially robotics, care, construction, infrastructure and research. That is the hypothesis this model exposes—not a conclusion supported by those sources.

Why the model does not claim to have solved resilience

The GDP calculations do not estimate the distribution of income, care quality, actual housing costs, emissions, public trust or debt sustainability. A large GDP gain could still coincide with mass displacement or fiscal pressure. The model has to pass separate social and physical tests before any pathway should be called successful.

Capital cost: a deliberately unanswered question

The four cases assume illustrative additional annual public-and-private investment intentions of 0%, 0.7%, 1.5% and 3% of GDP at full scale, respectively. At the default settings in the fourth pathway, 3% of the modelled 2035 GDP would be roughly A$232 billion per year in 2026 purchasing-power terms—public and private combined. This is an extraordinary capital commitment and is not included in the calculated outputs or independently costed. A proper macroeconomic model would test the effect on energy, construction prices, interest rates, import dependence, public debt and private consumption. Until then, no GDP gain can be described as available government revenue or resilience funding.

Critical potential failure: Exponential improvement in measured AI capability does not prove that the economic production frontier will expand by the same factor. The required constraints include value of additional output, scarcity of physical inputs, rebound demand, environmental limits, safety, income distribution and policy choices. Scenario 4 should be downgraded if its empirical gates fail—even if benchmark capability keeps doubling.

Suggested numerical go/no-go tests (not published forecasts)

Pre-register measurable thresholds in three diverse Australian pilots during 2027–2029. These are proposed decision criteria, not estimates of what the pilots will achieve:

PilotTestContinue only if…
Care operationsCompare AI-enabled administration and care scheduling against matched non-AI services for 12 months.At least 20% fewer admin hours per client, with no worsening in safety, client outcomes or unpaid caregiver hours.
Housing productionTest AI planning/design plus modern off-site production against closely matched developments.At least 25% greater quality-adjusted output per labour hour and evidence of lower delivered housing costs, not just land-price inflation.
Regional SMEsRandomise access to AI workflow redesign and capital support, measuring value added—not only task speed.At least 20% greater quality-adjusted value added per hour net of AI subscriptions, integration, energy and error-correction costs.

If these results are not replicated in independent settings, reduce assumptions or reject the aggressive 2035 diffusion timetable. Passing them would support more ambitious modelling; it would not validate a 90% economy-wide AI productivity uplift.

Minimum independent data for a credible second version

ABS: annual capital inputs, industry output and hours, real GDP and demographic series. ATO/Treasury: income distributions, effective taxes, investment and budget flows. AI: audited task benchmarks plus industry adoption and net output. JSA/AIHW/NDIA: paid and unpaid care needs, workforce shifts, outcomes and cost. Housing: completions, land prices, location, rents and time-to-approval. Climate: geolocated asset risk and expected annual damage.

Model development should add (a) capital stocks and depreciation; (b) sector input–output relations; (c) cohort-based work, health and care; (d) income and ownership distribution; (e) climate damage and adaptation; and (f) uncertainty ranges and behavioural feedback. A single-factor GDP extrapolation cannot replace those modules.

Next milestone: Test whether pathway 4 can pass one integrated 2027–2029 steel thread: AI-assisted housing and care delivery, connected to workforce hours, productive capital, quality outcomes and public investment costs. If gains are not measurable at programme scale, reduce the model's 2035 assumptions.
